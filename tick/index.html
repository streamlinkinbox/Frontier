Field Guide · giant hard tick ♀
Real tick biology, scaled ×30 into a pack-raiding race parasite.

1 · Questing — the front legs are a nose

Ticks can't jump, fly, or drop from trees. They climb vegetation, grip with the rear pairs, and extend the front pair — waving the Haller's organ, a sensory pit on the first tarsi that detects CO₂, heat, humidity and vibration from metres away. When the host's plume hits, the tick orients and marches. Watch pair I lift the moment you press CO₂.

Sonenshine & Anderson (tick biology) · Doctronic/MegaBee reviews of Haller's organ · pest-control literature consensus

2 · Attachment — cut, harpoon, cement

The chelicerae lacerate the surface, then the hypostome — a shaft lined with backward (retrorse) barbs — drives in. Salivary glands then secrete a glycine-rich protein cement that gels into a cone around the mouthparts. Barbs + cement = dual fixation that resists scraping for days. That's why your wall-scrape must be violent — and why withdrawal rocks side to side.

Moorhouse 1969 · Kemp et al. 1982 · Mulenga et al. 2022 (cement proteins) · PMC 2026 mini-review of fixation

3 · Engorgement — the scutum never grows

An adult female feeds once for days and balloons to many times her unfed mass (up to ~100× by weight). Only the soft alloscutum expands — the hard scutum shield keeps its exact size, ending up as a small plate on a giant sac. The rig reproduces this exactly: scutum fixed, sac ×2.5, wrinkles smoothing as it stretches.

Ixodidae overview (ScienceDirect) · Sonenshine & Anderson 2013

4 · Eight legs — alternating tetrapod

Like spiders, ticks walk four legs at a time: (L1·R2·L3·R4) ⇄ (R1·L2·R3·L4), shifting toward a metachronal wave at low speed. Stance feet stay planted while the body travels — including up the bumper face, where “down” is the panel normal and the claws do the work.

Arachnid locomotion literature · analogous hexapod continuum (Cruse/Walknet)

5 · The duct breach (game adaptation)

Real ticks crawl to sheltered feeding sites (ears, groin, armpits — warm, thin, hidden). The air duct is the car's armpit: dark, warm from the pack, and a straight highway past the bodywork. The tick crouches to tunnel height, folds its workspace, and siphons the cells until replete — then lets go and drops.
